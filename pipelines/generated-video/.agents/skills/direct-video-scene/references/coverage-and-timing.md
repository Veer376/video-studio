# Coverage and timing

Coverage decides how many views the audience needs and how those views unfold over the available duration. Use the least coverage that makes the event clear and effective.

## Choose the architecture

### Single stable composition

Use when blocking, performance, transformation, or graphic change is already readable from one view. Stillness is valid.

### Single evolving shot

Use when one continuous camera behavior can reveal or reframe the event without losing spatial clarity.

### Master plus detail or reaction

Establish the relationship, then cut to the information that becomes newly important: a face, hand, object, interface state, or consequence.

### Shot / reverse shot

Use when alternating attention between participants is essential. Preserve the interaction axis, eyelines, and enough temporal space for listening and reaction.

### Reveal structure

Begin with incomplete information and change framing, focus, blocking, or viewpoint only when the reveal should register.

### Action coverage

Prioritize geography and causality: establish where the action happens, show decisive preparation/contact, and show consequence. Avoid excessive cuts that make direction, ownership, or impact ambiguous.

### Montage

Use when compression, comparison, accumulation, repetition, or rhythmic escalation is itself the purpose. Each image should contribute new information.

### Deterministic graphic progression

For interfaces, diagrams, motion graphics, or constructed graphics, treat state changes as beats: establish context, perform the change, let it read, then hand attention to the next state.

## Clip boundaries are not dramatic boundaries

A short clip does not need its own beginning-middle-end story. It may contain only:

- the continuation of an action begun earlier;
- a reaction;
- one reveal;
- one explanatory state change;
- one held emotional or visual condition;
- the setup for a later payoff.

Judge the clip by whether it fulfills its contribution to the larger sequence.

## Build the timing envelope

Budget time for what the viewer must actually perceive:

- orientation before complex movement when geography is new;
- preparation before contact when cause matters;
- readable contact/state change;
- reaction or consequence;
- spoken lines and listening;
- text/UI reading time where applicable;
- an ending hold when the edit or next clip needs it.

Do not assign timestamps to every gesture by default. Use precise spans when synchronization or density requires them; otherwise use coarse phases.

## Viewer priority

At any instant, identify the dominant thing the viewer should read. Simultaneous events are acceptable when they form one relationship, such as a subject acting while another reacts. Competing unrelated actions create ambiguity.

When attention must hand off, create a visible cue: movement stops, a gaze shifts, sound occurs, focus changes, a new object enters, or the camera reframes.

## Holds

Motion does not need to fill the entire clip. Hold when the viewer needs to:

- recognize a face or object;
- understand a result;
- absorb dialogue;
- read text or interface state;
- feel a reaction;
- prepare for a cut.

An intentional hold is different from dead time: attention remains active even if the camera is still.

## Cuts and transitions

Use a cut when a new view communicates something more clearly than continuous movement. Common purposes include:

- change of information source;
- reaction;
- spatial clarification;
- time compression;
- contrast;
- matching action, shape, movement, or sound.

Do not add transitions merely for variety. A hard cut is often the clearest transition.

## Density gate

Reject or simplify a plan when:

- essential actions cannot finish in time;
- dialogue consumes the time needed for visible reaction;
- the camera must perform several unrelated moves;
- cuts become too short to read;
- action, text, and sound demand attention simultaneously without hierarchy;
- the planned ending state cannot be reached naturally.

Remove decorative movement, redundant coverage, background action, or nonessential sound before weakening a locked story beat.
