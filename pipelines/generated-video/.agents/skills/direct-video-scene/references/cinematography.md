# Cinematography

Cinematography controls what information is available, what receives emphasis, and how the viewer relates spatially and emotionally to the event.

## Begin with viewer information

Before choosing a lens term or camera move, answer:

- What must be visible now?
- What should remain hidden or secondary?
- Is the important information a face, body relationship, object detail, environment, action path, or transformation?
- Does the viewer need objectivity, intimacy, subjectivity, scale, pressure, uncertainty, or spatial clarity?

Choose the simplest view that communicates the intended information.

## Shot size

- **Extreme / very wide:** environment, isolation, scale, geography.
- **Wide / full:** body action, paths, multiple subjects, subject-in-environment relationships.
- **Medium:** interaction, dialogue, gesture, prop handling.
- **Medium close / close:** reaction, attention, thought, intimate detail.
- **Extreme close / insert:** a decisive feature, object, contact point, or small state change.

Shot size is a communication choice, not a decoration. If the action depends on feet, hands, object placement, or multiple bodies, do not frame so tightly that the cause disappears.

## Angle and viewpoint

Use camera height and angle to change readable relationships:

- eye-level for direct observation;
- low or high angle when relative power, vulnerability, geometry, or scale actually matters;
- over-the-shoulder for a clear interpersonal axis;
- point-of-view for subjective information;
- overhead for choreography, layout, isolation, or pattern;
- oblique/canted framing only when instability or unusual geometry is part of the intended effect.

Avoid assigning symbolic meaning automatically. The visible relationship should justify the choice.

## Camera position and movement

Treat the camera as a physical observer with:

- a starting position;
- an orientation and height;
- a support/behavior: static, handheld, stabilized, mounted, virtual, etc.;
- an optional path;
- a stopping point or final composition.

Distinguish **rotation** from **translation**:

- pan/tilt rotates from a position;
- dolly/truck/crane translates through space;
- zoom changes field of view without moving the camera.

This distinction matters because each changes parallax, background relationships, and spatial comprehension differently.

## Motivate movement

A camera move should change at least one of:

- information revealed;
- relationship between subjects;
- perceived scale or isolation;
- tension or intimacy;
- viewer attention;
- spatial comprehension.

If nothing meaningful changes, hold the camera.

For multi-phase moves, state the sequence clearly: follow while the subject crosses, hold when the subject stops, then push in when the new information lands. Do not collapse incompatible movements into one vague instruction.

## Composition and depth

- Preserve readable silhouettes, faces, hands, and important props.
- Use foreground, subject plane, and background to create depth when it improves spatial or emotional reading.
- Preserve looking room and movement room unless deliberate pressure requires imbalance.
- Use negative space for absence, expectation, direction, or off-screen presence.
- Establish emphasis through blocking, contrast, focus, light, and movement before adding decorative detail.
- Treat the ending composition as a designed state, especially when the next clip inherits it.

## Focus and lens character

Specify focal behavior when it affects attention: deep focus for spatial relationships, shallow focus for isolation, a rack of focus for an attention handoff.

Use focal-length or capture terminology only when the visible consequence matters: exaggerated depth, compression, distortion, intimacy, detached observation, or shallow focus. Do not add arbitrary millimeter, aperture, shutter, or frame-rate values merely to sound cinematic.

## Lighting and visual emphasis

Lighting direction belongs here when it affects readability or the scene's established grammar. Describe source and visible consequence: window light isolates a face; a practical lamp creates a warm pool within a cooler room; backlight separates rain from the background.

Do not redesign a locked location or project look inside a single clip. Local treatment should inherit the established visual system unless the story requires a change.

## Deterministic and motion-graphic scenes

The same principles apply to a virtual camera:

- establish the visual hierarchy;
- move to reveal causality, hierarchy, or relationship;
- let important state changes complete while the viewer can read them;
- use holds deliberately;
- finish on a stable composition when the edit needs a clean handoff.
