# Sound direction

Sound controls attention, timing, physical credibility, atmosphere, and transitions. Direct it as part of the scene when it materially affects the viewer experience.

## Sound layers

Use only the layers the scene needs:

1. **Speech:** dialogue, voiceover, exertion, breath, or deliberate vocal silence.
2. **Synchronized effects:** contact, footsteps, cloth, mechanisms, objects, doors, impacts, interface cues, or environmental reactions tied to visible events.
3. **Ambience:** the stable acoustic identity of place and time.
4. **Music:** rhythm, emotional structure, escalation, release, contrast, or deliberate absence.

State priority when layers compete.

## Locked audio can lead the direction

When exact dialogue, narration, music, or an existing cue has fixed timing, establish that timing before finalizing coverage. The visual plan must leave enough time for important words, reactions, and sound events to register.

Do not treat sound as a universal final-pass decoration.

## Speech and listening

- Preserve exact locked wording.
- Identify the audible source when it matters.
- Direct delivery only to the degree supported by the scene: pace, restraint, breath, interruption, distance, or emotional pressure.
- Account for what the listener is doing while speech occurs.
- Leave acoustic and visual space around an important line or reaction.

Do not add dialogue, vocalization, or voiceover that the source does not authorize.

## Synchronized effects

Tie important sound to visible cause:

`preparation → contact → resonance/decay → reaction`.

A synchronized effect can make action feel physical, mark an attention handoff, punctuate a reveal, or motivate a reaction.

## Ambience and acoustic space

Ambience should establish the environment rather than act as generic noise. Consider distance, room size, surfaces, weather, machinery, crowds, or exterior activity when those qualities materially affect the scene.

Do not describe every possible environmental sound. Preserve a clear hierarchy.

## Music

Use music when it contributes structure, rhythm, contrast, or a project-level motif. If the score is not generated with the shot, the directing contract may still state the intended musical function or sync point while leaving implementation downstream.

Avoid generic `cinematic music` as a substitute for a specific directorial purpose.

## Silence

Silence can be an active decision: remove competing layers so a look, pause, text reveal, or physical event carries the moment. Distinguish narrative silence from the downstream technical requirement to remove an audio stream.

## Sound perspective and off-screen space

Off-screen sound can reveal presence, direction, distance, or an event beyond the frame. Use it when the source and narrative purpose are understandable.

Sound perspective should match the camera/listener relationship unless an intentional subjective treatment overrides it.

## Bridges and transitions

Sound can connect clips through:

- an effect that begins before a cut or decays after it;
- ambience carried across a visual change;
- dialogue continuing across coverage;
- music entering or leaving at a structural beat;
- deliberate abrupt silence for contrast.

When the next clip depends on an audio tail, include that state in the continuity handoff.
