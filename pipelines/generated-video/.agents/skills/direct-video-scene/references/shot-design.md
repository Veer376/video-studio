# Shot design

Choose shots by narrative function. A shot term is useful only when it changes what the viewer sees, understands, or feels.

## Shot size

| Shot | Best use | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme close-up or insert | A decisive eye movement, hand action, clue, texture, or object state | Withholds geography and other characters |
| Close-up | Emotion, realization, restrained dialogue, or a reaction that carries the beat | Requires a clear eyeline and uncluttered background |
| Medium close-up | Dialogue with facial expression and some gesture | Can feel static unless blocking or camera behaviour develops |
| Medium shot | Conversation, hand gestures, and character-to-prop interaction | Background and co-character positions must stay readable |
| Full shot | Whole-body action, entrances, exits, costume, or physical relationship | Facial nuance becomes secondary |
| Wide shot | Geography, group relationships, isolation, scale, or coordinated action | Small gestures and subtle expressions may not register |
| Establishing wide | Location, time, atmosphere, and movement routes | Use briefly unless the environment itself is the subject |

## Angle and viewpoint

- **Eye level:** direct, observational, and emotionally neutral unless performance changes it.
- **Low angle:** authority, threat, monumentality, or vulnerability of the observer.
- **High angle:** exposure, fragility, spatial explanation, or loss of control.
- **Over-the-shoulder:** dialogue relationship, eyeline, and controlled reveal of the listener.
- **Point of view:** subjective discovery or danger; show what motivates the following reaction.
- **Profile or two-shot:** relationship, opposition, shared movement, or silence between characters.
- **Top-down:** pattern, choreography, confinement, geography, or object arrangement.

Use a motivated viewpoint. Do not alternate angles merely to appear cinematic.

## Camera behaviour

| Behaviour | Dramatic effect |
|---|---|
| Locked or static | Observation, restraint, tension, comedy through blocking, or emphasis on subject motion |
| Push in | Increasing attention, realization, threat, or intimacy |
| Pull back | Reveal context, isolation, consequence, or emotional distance |
| Pan or tilt | Reveal adjacent information or follow movement along one axis |
| Tracking or dolly | Stay physically connected to purposeful movement |
| Handheld | Instability, urgency, proximity, or documentary immediacy |
| Orbit | Reframe a relationship or heighten a suspended decisive moment |
| Rise, crane, or aerial reveal | Expand scale, conclude a sequence, or expose geography |
| Rack focus | Transfer attention between established foreground and background subjects |

Describe the camera's start, path, subject relationship, and end. Combining movements is justified only when their sequence remains visually legible.

## Shot architecture

### Single continuous shot

Use when one evolving action, performance, or reveal can carry the scene without missing necessary information. Let blocking or camera movement create progression inside the frame.

### Master with detail or reaction

Establish geography, then cut to the detail or reaction that changes the meaning. Each cut must contribute information unavailable in the previous framing.

### Shot and reverse shot

Use when turns of speech or reaction are the scene's engine. Preserve eyelines, screen direction, background logic, and the listener's active presence.

### Reveal structure

Begin with incomplete information, then use subject movement, camera movement, focus, or a cut to expose the important fact. The reveal must change the viewer's understanding.

### Action coverage

Establish where the action occurs, show the decisive cause or contact clearly, then show its consequence. Avoid cutting so frequently that direction and impact become unreadable.

### Montage

Use only when compression, comparison, accumulation, or rhythmic escalation is the purpose. Give each image a distinct contribution and a readable transition.

## Composition and depth

- Keep silhouettes, faces, hands, and important props visually separable.
- Use foreground, subject plane, and background to create depth only when all remain readable.
- Preserve looking room and movement room unless deliberate imbalance creates the intended pressure.
- Use negative space to imply absence, anticipation, scale, or an off-screen presence.
- Place visual emphasis through contrast, light, focus, movement, and blocking before adding decorative detail.
- Treat the ending composition as a handoff, not an accidental final frame.

Lens or capture terminology is optional. Specify it only when the visible effect—spatial exaggeration, compression, intimacy, distortion, or shallow focus—is important to the scene.
